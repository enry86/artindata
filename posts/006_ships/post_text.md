## Instagram caption
Every position in 1,736 ships' logbooks, 1750 to 1855: Dutch, British, Spanish and
French ships, about 260,000 days at sea. No land is drawn. The continents appear
on their own, as the places no ship went.

Look for the Cape of Good Hope, where nearly every voyage between Europe and Asia
squeezes through, the wide swing out towards Brazil to catch the trade winds,
the Dutch road to Java along the roaring forties, and the Spanish ships to Havana,
Veracruz and round Cape Horn.

It's drawn as a Mercator chart, the projection the navigators themselves used.

Swipe for the logbooks' own words: a mutiny, a tired bird kept in a basket, rats
in the powder room, pirates, and Cook's ship crossing the Antarctic Circle.

Data: CLIWOC 2.1, digitised logbooks from Dutch, British, Spanish and French
archives (PANGAEA, CC BY 3.0). Positions are 18th-century estimates; Dutch
notes are my translations. Full method and code: link in bio.

## Alt text · image 1
An old sea chart on aged cream parchment, framed by a black-and-white degree
border, with a faint grid, dashed tropics, the Equator and the Antarctic Circle.
A compass rose sits in the empty top right, with faint compass lines fanning out.
No coastlines are drawn. Instead, thousands of thin, translucent coloured threads,
the tracks of sailing ships, trace the oceans so densely that the shapes of the
continents appear as blank paper: Europe, Africa, South America and India. Teal
(Spanish) threads fill the Gulf of Mexico and run down the coast of South America
and round Cape Horn. A thick braid of orange (Dutch) and crimson (British) threads
swings west across the Atlantic towards Brazil, then sweeps east, pinches together
just south of Africa at the Cape of Good Hope, and fans out across the Indian Ocean
towards India and Java. Blue (French) threads crowd the North Atlantic. Title:
"Under sail". Subtitle: "Every position in 1,736 ships' logbooks, 1750–1855. No
land is drawn: the continents are where no ship went." A key at bottom left names
the four nations.

## Alt text · image 2
The same sea chart with six notes in italic, each joined by a thin line to a small
ring on a ship's logged position. Over Greenland: "At 1 am it cleared, saw the moon
eclipsed […] and 3 minutes past 2 she was total eclipse." (Prince Rupert, British,
30 Jun 1768). Over the Sahara: "Mutiny among the soldiers narrowly prevented. They
wanted to seize the ship, murder their opponents and sail to America." (Delphina,
Dutch, 18 Apr 1820). Over Asia: "Pirates" (Centurion, British, 27 Dec 1794, off
India). Over Africa: "The black land bird is worn out from flying and lets itself
be caught easily. It is put in a little basket in the gallery." (Tromp, Dutch,
11 May 1817). In the Indian Ocean: "Cartridges in the powder room gnawed by rats."
(Maria Reigersbergen, Dutch, 16 Sep 1816). Near the bottom: "Sea ice. James Cook's
ship is the first known to cross the Antarctic Circle." (Resolution, British,
17 Jan 1773).
